## 🧠 1. The intuition behind your statement

Occlusion sensitivity makes sense only if **occluding a small *region*** of the input corresponds to a *spatially local feature* in the model’s representation.

That means the input must have **spatial structure** — like an image matrix where nearby pixels belong together (e.g., strokes of the digit “3”).

If you flatten the image into a 1-D vector (length 784 for MNIST),
then each input feature (pixel) loses its *position* — pixel 123 and pixel 124 may be on opposite corners of the image, but the model no longer “knows” that.

So when you “occlude” (zero out) a 4×4 patch of pixels in the flattened vector,
you are really just turning off 16 unrelated features, not a coherent region of the image.
That completely breaks the visual meaning of occlusion maps.

---

## ⚙️ 2. When occlusion sensitivity **works** vs **fails**

| Model Input Representation                                                | Works for Occlusion Sensitivity? | Why                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **2D structured input** (image tensor, shape `(28, 28)` or `(28, 28, 1)`) | ✅ Yes                            | Occluding contiguous pixels simulates hiding part of the visual field.           |
| **Flattened 1D vector** (shape `(784,)`)                                  | ⚠️ No (meaningless spatially)    | Each feature is independent; no local patch concept.                             |
| **Sequence input** (like text tokens or time-series)                      | ⚙️ Partially                     | Works if occlusion is defined over contiguous subsequences, not random features. |

---

## 🧩 3. What happens in your project specifically

You’re absolutely right — some of your models (KNN, DecisionTree, NaiveBayes, MLP) expect **flattened inputs**, while the CNN expects **2D structured inputs**.

### So here’s what you did correctly in your notebook:

In your function `simple_importance_map_any()` you added a smart design:

```python
if model_name in {'KNN', 'NaiveBayes'}:
    window, stride = max(window, 7), min(stride, 2)
```

That means you *still* slide over the **2D 28×28 image**, even for those models — you never actually flatten it *before* occluding.
You reshape the occluded image into a 1-D vector **only when calling `predict_proba`**.

So the occlusion operates spatially, but the model sees a flattened version.
That’s the right compromise:
👉 the *perturbation* is spatial (2D),
👉 the *model evaluation* is as the model expects (1D input).

This way, you can still generate interpretable heatmaps even for models that don’t internally use spatial structure.

---

## 📉 4. Why it’s still limited for flattened models

Even though your implementation preserves the 2D occlusion **at the input stage**,
models like Decision Trees, MLPs, or Naive Bayes do **not use spatial context** internally.

So while the heatmap is drawn in 2D, what it’s really showing is:

> “Which *individual pixel values* (or small groups of pixel features) most influenced the classifier’s decision.”

not:

> “Which *spatial region* the model looked at.”

For CNNs, that distinction *does* correspond to meaningful image patches (e.g., top of digit “7”).
For non-CNNs, it’s more abstract — it’s just pixel indices that happened to matter most statistically.
